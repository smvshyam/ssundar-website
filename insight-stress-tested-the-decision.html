We Stress-Tested the Decision. The Decision Picked the Test.

OCTOBER 27, 2026 5 MIN READ

By the time someone says let us stress-test this, the decision has already been made.

You know the ritual because you have performed it. A proposal has been walked through, the deck is polished, the sponsor has stopped presenting and started nodding, and the room has the particular warmth of a thing about to be agreed. Then, almost as a courtesy to rigor, someone suggests that a person play devil's advocate. A volunteer is found, or more often volunteered. They raise two or three concerns. Each concern is received with visible respect and answered with a sentence that begins, that is a fair point, and. The concerns are noted. The decision proceeds. Everyone leaves the room feeling they did the responsible thing, because they can now say the idea was challenged before it was approved.

It was not challenged. It was processed.

The challenge that reports to the decision.

Look at who is holding the knife. The person asked to argue against the decision was selected by the person who wants the decision, scheduled by them, and will need their goodwill on the next three things they work on. You have asked someone to kill an idea in public and then go back to depending on the person whose idea it was. They understand the assignment perfectly, which is why they argue with exactly enough force to be seen arguing and not one degree more. The performance of dissent is the whole deliverable. Actual dissent was never on the agenda, and everyone in the room has known this since before the meeting began.

Then there is the timing, which is not an accident. The challenge is scheduled for the end, after the decision has set emotionally even if it has not set formally. By the time the devil's advocate speaks, reversing course would mean unwinding an hour of shared momentum, contradicting the senior person who has begun to lean, and telling the sponsor that the work they just presented was wrong. The objection is admitted into the room at precisely the moment it has the least possible power to change anything. This is not a flaw in the ritual. It is the function of the ritual. A challenge that could actually stop the decision would have to arrive earlier, and earlier is exactly where it is never placed.

The language gives the game away. We play devil's advocate. We play. It is a role, a costume pulled on for a few minutes and hung back up when the exercise is done. A real objection does not have to be assigned to anyone, because a person who genuinely believes the decision is wrong does not need a turn allocated to them in order to say so. The moment challenge has to be appointed, you have already learned the thing that matters, which is that it would not have arisen on its own. The room had closed before the challenge was scheduled. The appointment is not the opening of a question. It is a small funeral for one.

What the exercise produces is not scrutiny. It is immunity. The organization believes it has bought insurance against being blindsided, and the policy has been underwritten by the very decision it is meant to interrogate. Worse, the ritual leaves a residue that outlasts the meeting. From that day forward the decision carries a certificate: we pressure-tested this, we had someone argue the other side, we did our due diligence. The certificate does not make the decision better. It makes it harder to reopen, because reopening it now means challenging not just the call but the visibly rigorous process that blessed it. You have manufactured armor and called it examination.

A challenge you appoint is not a test of the decision. It is a character witness for it.

Here is the reframe that should sit badly. The purpose of the appointed challenger was never to find the flaw. It was to be able to say, afterward, that you looked. Organizations do not stage dissent in order to change decisions. They stage it in order to legitimize them, and the two goals are not merely different, they are opposed. The more elaborate the apparatus of challenge becomes, the pre-mortem, the red team, the dedicated skeptic, the structured pushback session, the more legitimacy the decision accumulates and the less anyone can lay a hand on it afterward. You did not build a mechanism that catches bad decisions before they ship. You built a mechanism that launders confident ones into unquestionable ones.

What challenge costs when it is real.

Real challenge cannot be a role, because a role can be revoked by the person who assigned it. It has to be a structure, and structures are expensive in the one currency organizations hoard most carefully, which is control. The person who challenges a decision cannot depend on the person who makes it, which means their standing, their next assignment, and their review cannot sit in the decider's hands. The challenge has to arrive before the decision sets, when changing course still costs an argument rather than a reorganization. And someone in the room has to hold not a microphone but a brake, the actual authority to halt the thing, not merely to have their concern recorded in the minutes. Most organizations are glad to hand out the microphone. Almost none will give up the brake, because the brake is the part that would make the challenge real, and a real challenge might take the decision away from the person who wanted it.

This is the pattern SSUNDAR's simulations tend to expose, and it is remarkably stubborn. Put a capable leader under pressure, give them a decision and a crisis stacking against a running clock, and watch what they do with dissent. The strong ones run the ritual immaculately. They invite the pushback, they thank the person for it, they reflect it back accurately, and then they proceed exactly as they would have proceeded anyway, entirely unaware that nothing the challenger said was ever allowed to land. They cannot tell the difference between having been challenged and having felt challenged, and the organization around them cannot either, because the two are identical from the outside and only one of them has a brake attached. The leaders who are genuinely dangerous to a bad idea are the ones who went looking, before the room warmed, for the person who would tell them they were wrong and could afford to mean it.

You did not want to be challenged. You wanted to have been.
