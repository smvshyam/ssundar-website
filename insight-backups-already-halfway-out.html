SYSTEMS ARCHITECTURE

Every Seat Had a Named Backup. Half the Backups Were Already Halfway Out the Door.

NOVEMBER 27, 2026 5 MIN READ

The succession grid came up on the screen at one hundred percent. Every critical seat had a name in the box beside it, and every box was filled, which is the only number this review has ever been asked to produce. The CHRO moved through it the way these reviews always move, seat by seat, name by name, a nod and a next slide. Coverage: complete. Risk: mitigated. The deck closed on a green row and a line about the bench being the strongest it had been in three years.

Nobody in the room had asked the only question the grid could not answer on its own: how many of the names in that second column were themselves already somewhere else in their heads. Three of the named backups had updated their LinkedIn headlines that quarter. One had quietly interviewed for a lateral move at a competitor and been turned down on compensation, not on interest. Two were sitting in their own manager's succession conversation, as a flight risk, in a document this review never crosses paths with. The grid called all of them covered. The grid had no column for that.

A named backup is a fact about today. The review treats it as a fact about the year.

Succession planning, as most organizations run it, is an annual act of naming. Someone sits with each critical role and writes a second name beside the first, sometimes a third. The exercise feels rigorous because it produces a document, and the document produces a number, and the number is the one the board wants to hear: percentage of critical roles with an identified successor. It is a clean metric. It is also, on its own, close to meaningless, because it answers whether a name exists and says nothing about whether that name will still be answering the phone when it is needed.

A name written into a box in March is being treated, silently, as durable through the following March. Nobody states this assumption out loud, because stated plainly it sounds absurd. Of course a person's situation changes over a year. Of course engagement rises and falls, offers arrive, managers change, a reorg moves someone sideways into a role they didn't choose. The grid knows all of this is true in general and accounts for none of it in particular, because the only instrument it has is a cell that holds a name, and a name does not expire visibly. It just sits there, true until proven false, usually on the day the primary resigns and the backup is quietly checked and found to have left four months earlier.

The plan optimizes for the review, not for the Tuesday it is needed.

Here is what the ritual actually produces. It produces a document that performs well in the one meeting it was built for, the annual talent review, and degrades continuously in every week after that meeting until the next one. Coverage is highest on the day the grid is signed and lowest on the day before it is updated again, and nobody is tracking that decay because nobody owns the gap between review cycles. HR owns the annual exercise. The business owns whether the backup actually shows up when called. The twelve months in between belong to no one, and it is precisely in those twelve months that a named backup quietly stops being one.

This is not a story about bad succession planning. The organizations running this ritual are, by most measures, running it well. The documents are thorough, the conversations are honest, the coverage numbers are real on the day they are measured. The failure is not in the rigor of the annual act. It is in treating an annual act as though it produces a fact that holds for the other three hundred and sixty-four days, when the only thing that actually holds for all three hundred and sixty-four is the question of whether that backup's own trajectory is still pointed toward staying.

One hundred percent coverage measured in March and never checked again is not a safety net. It is a photograph of a safety net, taken once, trusted all year.

Forecast the backup's own trajectory, not just their name.

The fix is not a more thorough annual review. More rigor applied once a year to a number that decays weekly just produces a more detailed photograph. The fix is to stop treating the successor name as a static fact and start forecasting it the way any other perishable capability should be forecast: continuously, from signals the organization already holds and rarely connects to the succession grid at all. Tenure trajectory in the backup's current role. Time since their last promotion or stretch assignment, which predicts restlessness better than almost any survey question does. Whether they are, themselves, named as someone else's backup, which spreads them thinner than the grid admits. Whether their own manager flagged them as a retention risk in a completely separate conversation the succession review never reads.

None of this requires new data collection. It requires connecting three documents that already exist in three different owners' hands, and reading the connection as a trend rather than a quarterly snapshot. A backup whose tenure-in-role is climbing past the point where internal promotion usually arrives, who has gone two review cycles without a stretch assignment, and who surfaced as a flight risk in someone else's retention conversation is not a green cell. The grid currently has no way to say so, because green and red in a succession document mean nothing more than named and unnamed.

This is the same discipline under a different label: trajectory over snapshot, applied to the bench the organization believes it already has, not only the bench it is still building. A coverage number that forecasts forward, weighted by how durable each named successor actually is this quarter, is a different number from the one that currently reaches the board, and it is usually a smaller one. That is not a reason to hide it. A capability that is priced honestly, including the names that are quietly at risk, is worth more to the organization that acts on it than a coverage number that is comfortable and wrong.

Where this breaks.

A trajectory forecast on a successor is still a probability, not a guarantee. It will not catch the backup who is poached overnight by an offer nobody saw coming, or the one who leaves for a reason that has nothing to do with the job at all. And the forecast itself has to stay inside the room that builds it. The moment a flagged successor learns they have been quietly scored as a flight risk, the forecast stops predicting behavior and starts causing it. This is a tool for the people who own the bench, not a score to hand the person sitting in the second column.

The grid said one hundred percent. By the time the first seat actually opened, the name beside it had already given notice somewhere else, and the only document that could have shown that coming was never built to look.
