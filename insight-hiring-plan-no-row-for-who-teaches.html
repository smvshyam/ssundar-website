The Hiring Plan Had a Row for Every Seat. Nobody Counted Who Would Teach Them.

NOVEMBER 23, 2026 6 MIN READ

The post-mortem on the missed ramp ran for forty minutes and found nothing wrong. Hiring had delivered the class on the date in the plan. The tools were live, the content was approved, the seats were allocated. The only line anyone could find to explain why so much of the new cohort was still off live work in the target week sat in the root-cause box at the bottom of the deck, in a smaller font than the headcount. It said "training capacity constrained."

Nobody had been negligent. The plan was a good plan, and every number on it had an owner. Everyone had counted the people who would sit in the room. Nobody had counted the people who would teach them, or the people who would sit beside them for the first month on the floor.

The plan counted the students. The constraint was the teachers.

A hiring plan is a schedule of arrivals. Class dates, seat counts, cost per head, start dates. It is precise about the one thing the hiring team controls, which is how many people walk in on Monday. What it never contains is the supply on the other side of the room: the trainers free that week, the floor mentors with hours to spare, the quality reviewers who can calibrate a new agent's first stretch of live contacts, the nesting buddies who are not also carrying their own targets.

That supply is the real speed limit on how many people can become proficient in a given month. A class of any size can be hired in a week. It can only be made good at the rate the people teaching it can be spared from the work they are already doing.

Because that capacity has no row, it is allocated by whoever asks first. And peak is the season when everyone asks at once. The same experienced people who would have been mentors are the ones the queue needs on the phones. Every one of those decisions is reasonable. Put them in sequence and a cohort that was funded, hired, equipped and scheduled is quietly left to teach itself.

The failure was reported after the miss, in a box at the bottom.

Look at when the constraint first appears in writing. It appears in the post-mortem, as a root cause, which is the grammar of the past tense. It sounds like a diagnosis. It is an obituary.

The weekly ramp report in the weeks before had said "on track." On track against what? Classes completed. Modules passed. Completion is a measure of the thing hiring and the training team control directly. Proficiency is a measure of the thing the teachers control, and the dashboard was built to watch the first and be surprised by the second. The data to see it coming was never missing. It was being produced every day by the operation, in handling times and quality scores, and nobody was asked to read it against the cohort that came before.

A ramp plan that counts arrivals but not teaching hours is a forecast of how many people will be hired, filed under how many will be ready.

Tell the delivery owner in January what March's teachers can carry.

Move the same sentence forward in time. Ninety days before the class lands, the delivery owner receives one page. For each cohort due, how many of them can reach threshold by the date demand arrives, given the teaching and mentoring hours that actually exist, and which weeks those hours collide with the peak. Not what the business needs. What the supply can carry. It names, in advance, who will be ready and who will not, while there is still time to do something about it.

Three mechanics make the page possible without a new system.

First, trajectory rather than snapshot. The page does not report who finished the course. It reports each cohort's handling and quality curve against the previous cohort's at the same week of tenure. A cohort whose curve flattens in week three is telling you the teaching supply has thinned. It says so before anyone files a root cause, from data the operation already holds. Nobody types it in, so nobody can flatter it.

Second, economy by exception. Most cohorts will be fine and cheap signals will say so. The expensive analysis is reserved for the places where two signals disagree: a cohort with perfect completion and a flat handling curve, or a cohort with healthy scores sitting on thin mentor cover. Go and sit with those people. Keep the indicator, discard the raw material, and the technology team has nothing to object to, because there is no new data store and no new burden on them.

Third, work backwards from the buyer. The same forecast is a different sentence at different altitudes. To the delivery owner it is revenue at risk in a named week. To finance it is the margin on a seat that is paid for and not yet productive. To the technology lead it is a promise of nothing new to build. To the executive sponsor it is the answer to whether the organisation can honestly commit to the peak.

Teaching hours belong on the price list, not only the cost line.

Look at how teaching capacity is booked. As overhead. It is the first thing that gets borrowed in a crunch, precisely because it looks like cost and nothing else on the sheet argues for it. Yet every week a cohort reaches proficiency earlier is a week a seat produces at full rate, and it is a week of risk the delivery owner does not carry.

That is a thing that can be priced. A date by which a seat is productive is a commitment a buyer can hold you to. A ramp commitment, stated at the point of sale and backed by teaching capacity that has been shown to exist, is a different offer from a headcount commitment, and the buyer can compare the two. Capacity that never reaches the price list is booked as cost forever, and the people closest to it will keep losing the argument to the queue.

Where this breaks.

It is worth being plain about the limits. A forecast of teaching hours is only as good as the discipline of the roster behind it, and rosters move. Demand in a real peak can exceed any plan, and no page will create mentors that the floor cannot spare. The mentors themselves leave, and a forecast that ignores that is a forecast of the best case. Small or newly formed cohorts produce noisy curves. And the slope can tell you that teaching has thinned without telling you why: it might be hours, or content, or tools, and somebody still has to go and look.

There is a human limit too. The forecast sometimes says "we cannot hire that many that fast, and make them ready," and it says it in January, when nothing has gone wrong yet. That sentence is unpopular in January. It is much cheaper there than in the post-mortem.
