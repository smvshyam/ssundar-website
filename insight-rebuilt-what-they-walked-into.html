We Didn't Train Them. We Rebuilt What They Walked Into.

AUGUST 10, 2026 9 MIN READ

The request arrives already diagnosed. It usually does. Someone senior has watched a behaviour they do not like repeat itself across enough people, for long enough, that it has stopped looking like a set of individuals and started looking like a type. The managers will not take ownership. The leaders escalate everything. The new heads freeze in their first hard quarter. And because the pattern is now visible at scale, the conclusion feels self-evident. These people need to be trained. A programme is scoped, a budget is found, a vendor is called, and the brief lands on the desk fully formed, with the solution already written into the problem statement.

We have taken that call more times than is comfortable to admit, and it always carries the same quiet certainty. The client is not asking whether training is the answer. They have decided that it is. What they want to know is how long it will take and what it will cost. The single most useful thing we do in the first week is almost always to decline the question as it was asked.

Every Organisation Runs Two Curricula.

Because when you go and watch the people who are supposedly failing, they are rarely failing. They are responding. The manager who will not take ownership works inside a structure where every independent decision she has ever made was either quietly overturned in a review she was not invited to, or held against her the one time it went wrong. She has learned the lesson the environment actually teaches, which is that ownership is a liability with no matching authority attached. She is not untrained. She is superbly trained, by the organisation, in precisely the behaviour the organisation rewards.

This is the part the brief never contains. Every company runs two curricula at once. There is the official one, delivered in workshops and e-learning modules and the competency framework sitting on a shared drive. And there is the real one, delivered every single day through what gets approved, what gets punished, who gets promoted, and how many meetings a decision has to survive before it counts as made. The official curriculum runs for two days a quarter. The real one runs continuously, and it never breaks for lunch. When the two disagree, there has never once been a contest. The environment wins before the workshop has finished setting up the projector.

So the training does exactly what training does under those conditions. It generates energy in the room, real energy, nobody is faking the insight. Then the same people walk back out through the same door they came in, into the same set of incentives that produced the behaviour in the first place, and inside a fortnight the environment has quietly retrained them back to baseline. The organisation watches this happen and reaches the most convenient conclusion available, which is that the participants lacked the discipline to apply what they learned. A follow-up module is commissioned. The follow-up will lose to the environment too. Everything loses to the environment. Losing to it is what makes it the environment.

The Escalation Was Never a Skills Gap.

One group came to us certain their mid-level leaders had an escalation problem. Everything travelled upward. The chief operating officer's calendar had become a queue of decisions that people two and three levels below him were entirely equipped to make. The request was for a decision-making programme. Teach them to decide. We asked to see something other than a training need. We asked for the last forty things that had been escalated, and, separately, for what had happened to the handful of people who had tried not to escalate.

The answer was in the second list, not the first. The ones who had decided on their own had, almost without exception, been asked afterward to account for themselves in a forum where being right was not a defence and being unlucky was fatal. Escalation was not a gap in anyone's capability. It was the only rational move left on the board. These were not leaders who could not decide. They were leaders who had run the numbers on what deciding cost them, and had come, correctly, to the same answer every time. You could have put every one of them through the finest decision-making course on earth and the calendar would have refilled by the end of the month, because the course was aimed at a deficiency that did not exist.

Here is the reframe the brief pays us to resist. You cannot train a person out of a system that is training them the other way every morning. It is not that training is weak. It is that it has been aimed at the wrong object entirely. The behaviour you dislike does not live inside your people. It lives inside the architecture your people walk into, and it will reappear in the next cohort, and the one after that, no matter how many of the current occupants you develop or replace, for exactly as long as the architecture stays intact. You are not looking at a population of individuals who need fixing. You are looking at a machine that is producing this output on purpose, and doing it reliably, which is the one thing everyone agrees a machine should do.

We Rebuilt the Door, Not the People.

So we did not train them. We rebuilt what they walked into. Not the people. The door. With that group it meant redrawing the point at which a decision was permitted to die instead of climbing, and, far harder, dismantling the after-the-fact tribunal that had made climbing the safe choice in the first place. It meant changing what a manager had to survive in order to make a call, so that making the call stopped being the professionally dangerous option it had quietly become. None of this appeared on a course catalogue. The escalations that used to fill the chief operating officer's calendar began to thin inside a quarter, and not one person had been sent on a workshop. The behaviour changed because the thing manufacturing the behaviour changed. The people were the same people. They had always been capable of it. They had simply, and rationally, never been safe to be.

This is most of what SSUNDAR actually does, and most of it happens before anything gets built. We treat the request for training as a symptom to be read rather than an instruction to be filled. We map the real curriculum, the one nobody wrote down, the one running through the approvals and the incentives and the meetings a decision has to clear before it exists. Then we rebuild that, because it is the only layer at which behaviour is genuinely decided. It is slower than a workshop and it photographs far worse. There is no room full of people holding certificates at the end. There is only the quiet, unglamorous fact of the behaviour no longer occurring, and the faintly eerie silence of a problem that has stopped recurring instead of being re-addressed every year.

The organisations that keep buying training are not wrong about the behaviour. They are wrong about its address. They keep posting letters to the occupants when the resident they are furious with is the building itself. Rebuild the building and the behaviour you have spent five years trying to train away disappears inside one, without a single person having attended anything at all.

You cannot coach a person out of a room that teaches them the opposite lesson every day they walk into it. The room was always the curriculum.

Your people already learned exactly what your organisation taught them. The only open question is whether you meant to teach it.
