The Decision Was Defensible. That Was the Problem.

OCTOBER 13, 2026 5 MIN READ

The hardest call on the agenda had two serious options, and the room spent most of its time on a third question that neither of them answered. Not which option was more likely to work. Which one would be easier to explain if it didn't. You could feel the conversation bend toward it. The option with the outside firm's name attached. The one the benchmark supported. The one the committee had already handled, so that no single person would have to stand behind it alone. By the time the call was made it had collected a remarkable amount of cover and almost no scrutiny of whether it was right.

This is the decision that reviews well and ages badly. Every box ticked. Every signature in place. A clean line of defense running from the choice all the way back to the process that produced it. If it fails, the post-mortem will find nothing to fault, because on paper nothing was done wrong. That is precisely the problem. The organization optimized the decision for the review that happens after it fails, and called that diligence.

Correctness and defensibility are two different targets.

A decision can be judged on two separate questions, and organizations routinely collapse them into one. The first is whether it was the best available call given what could be known at the time. The second is whether it can be defended afterward to someone who was never in the room. These are not the same question. The honest and slightly alarming truth is that they point in the same direction only by coincidence. Sometimes the most defensible option and the most correct one happen to be the same choice. The system is built as if they always are.

Watch what defensibility is actually made of and the cost comes into focus. It is built from precedent, because precedent means somebody else already took the risk of being first. From benchmarks, because conformity means you will have failed in good company. From external validation, because a logo absorbs blame that an internal judgment cannot. From consensus, because a decision everyone touched is a decision no one has to own. Each of these is a real insurance policy against being singled out. And each one, quietly, narrows the field of options to the conventional, the attributable, the already done. Defensibility is assembled out of precisely the things that make a decision average.

You can hear it happen in real time if you listen for the grammar. The question in the room slides from whether this will work to whether we can say we did the reasonable thing, and almost nobody marks the moment it changed. Someone asks what the market leaders are doing. Someone asks whether legal has seen it. Someone proposes a pilot, not to learn anything the team doesn't already suspect, but so that the full commitment can be described later as measured. Each intervention sounds like diligence and functions as insulation. The option that survives this is not the one that answered the question best. It is the one that gathered the most reasons that were never really reasons.

None of this is irrational on the part of the people doing it. It is the only sensible response to a system that inspects process and punishes exposure. A leader is rarely rewarded for an outcome they could not fully control. They are reliably punished for a decision they cannot account for. So they run the arithmetic anyone would run. They optimize for the thing the organization actually checks, which is not the quality of the judgment but the integrity of the paper trail. The incentive never asks them to be right. It asks them to be covered. They comply, because they are paying attention.

And so a strange inversion sets in. The better an organization gets at reviewing its decisions, the more fluently its people learn to make decisions that review well. Tighten the business-case template. Add an approval gate. Formalize the post-mortem. You have not made the underlying judgment any sharper. You have raised the premium on cover. Every control designed to improve the decision teaches the room, one more time, that the safe move is the defensible one. Management believes it is installing rigor. It is training a reflex to flinch toward the center of the room.

The post-mortem is already in the room.

A decision made to survive its own review is optimized for the wrong moment. The review happens after the outcome is already lost. The decision is the last place the outcome was still available.

Here is the part that is hard to sit with. The after-the-fact review is not a neutral audit of a choice that was made freely. It is a presence at the table in the moment of choosing, shaping the choice before anyone will admit it is there. Leaders decide with an invisible auditor in the room, and the auditor does not ask what is true. It asks what can be justified. So the organization believes it is measuring decision quality. What it is actually doing, every cycle, is selecting for the leaders most skilled at making choices that cannot later be criticized. That is a different skill from making good choices. Frequently it is the opposite one.

Reward the call, not the cover.

Rebuilding this is not a matter of courage posters or a speech about permission to fail, which are the usual substitutes for structure. It is about prying the two questions apart and refusing to let the second one contaminate the first. Ask what the best call is, on the merits, given what is knowable now, and get that answer on the record before anyone in the room is allowed to raise how it will be defended. Then, when the time comes to judge it, judge the reasoning at the moment it was made, not the outcome the world happened to hand back and not the thickness of the file behind it. Make it genuinely safe to have made an indefensible decision that was, on the evidence available at the time, correct. That single move does more than any gate ever added.

This is also the pattern that is nearly impossible to see from inside a resume and unmistakable the moment you put a leader under live pressure. When we run leaders through a cascading crisis where every option carries real exposure and there is no time to build a file, the tell is never who is decisive and who hesitates. It is who is solving for the problem and who has quietly started solving for the explanation they will owe afterward. Most never notice they have switched targets. The simulation notices, because it strips out the cover. There is no committee to stand inside, no precedent to borrow, no firm whose name you can put on the slide. There is only the call, and what the call reveals about what the person was actually optimizing for all along.

The most defensible decision in the room is usually the one nobody in it was willing to own.
