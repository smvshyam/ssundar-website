Nobody Decided This. It Became the Strategy Anyway.

OCTOBER 26, 2026 5 MIN READ

Somewhere in your organization is a strategy nobody chose.

You can find it in about four minutes. Walk into a planning meeting with someone who joined three weeks ago and watch them ask the one question the veterans have quietly trained themselves not to ask. Why do we do it this way? Not the process. The whole posture. Why do we route everything through that one vendor, why do we staff every build with contractors, why do we never ship anything in the first quarter. The room will not defend the practice. It will do something stranger. It will go quiet, and then someone will say that this is just how we do things here, and everyone will nod, relieved, as if the question has been answered when all that actually happened is that it was absorbed.

No one in that room decided the thing the newcomer is asking about. That is not a figure of speech. There was never a meeting, a memo, a deck, or a name attached to the choice. What there was instead was a sequence of small, reasonable, local calls, each one defensible on the day it was made, none of them large enough to trip the wire that would have summoned a real decision.

The wire that never tripped.

Every governance system a company builds is a tripwire. It waits for something to announce itself as a decision. A budget request, a proposal, a go or no-go, a sign-off. When the thing arrives wearing the costume of a decision, the machinery activates. Stakeholders are consulted, risks are logged, options are weighed. The system is good at this. It is proud of this.

It is also completely blind to anything that never puts on the costume. The vendor you now depend on entered as a ninety-day pilot that nobody got around to ending. The contractor-heavy model began as one urgent gap filled in a hurry, then a second, and then a hiring freeze made the workaround permanent without anyone ever proposing it as a model. The quarter you never ship in started as a single bad launch everyone silently agreed not to repeat. Each step cleared the bar for a small call. The sum of the steps became a strategy, and the strategy cleared no bar at all, because it never came up for a vote.

Then something worse happens. The accumulation hardens into identity. Ask the organization why it operates this way for long enough and it stops saying it is practical and starts saying it is who we are. We are a partner-led company. We stay lean. We are disciplined about timing. The drift gets a story, the story gets a culture, and the culture gets defended by people who would swear under oath that they chose it. They did not choose it. They inherited it from an earlier version of the company that was solving a different problem, under different constraints, with different people, and then they draped a value over the top so the inheritance would feel like a conviction.

A decision you can point to can be reversed. A decision that became a culture has to be survived.

This is not procrastination, and it is not the familiar story of a decision everyone avoided. Avoidance at least knows there is a decision to avoid. Drift does not. Nobody is dodging the call, because nobody experiences a call as pending. Every individual actor is behaving responsibly inside their own small frame. The failure is not moral and it is not laziness. It is architectural. There is simply no organ in the company whose job is to notice when a hundred local yeses have quietly added up to one enormous unexamined yes.

And the bill comes due at exactly the wrong moment. Because the practice was never framed as a choice, it was never given an expiry, a review date, or an owner who might one day say that this has outlived its reason. So it persists long past the conditions that once justified it. The urgent gap that created the contractor model closed years ago. The model remains. The partner who warranted an exclusive arrangement stopped being the best option. The arrangement remains. Unwinding any of it now is not treated as a decision either, because reversing a practice the company has wrapped into its own identity reads as betrayal rather than correction. The drift that cost nothing to enter costs a reorganization to leave.

Here is the part that should keep you up at night. The decisions your organization examines most carefully are almost never the ones that matter most. Your scrutiny is triggered by proposals, and your largest commitments did not arrive as proposals. They arrived as defaults. Which means the sharper and more disciplined your decision process becomes, the more completely it misses the choices that were never routed through it. You have built an immune system that only recognizes the pathogens polite enough to knock.

Auditing the choices nobody made.

Rebuilding this does not start with a better approval workflow. A better workflow only catches more of what already announces itself. It starts with the opposite instinct. A standing habit of asking, of every practice the organization treats as settled, when did we decide this, and what would we need to see in order to decide it again. Most of the time the honest answer is that no one decided it, and that answer is not an embarrassment. It is the most useful piece of information in the building. A practice nobody chose can be unchosen without anyone losing face, while a practice everyone believes they chose becomes a hill they will defend to the end.

The deeper fix is to grow leaders who can feel a decision that is not announcing itself. This is a specific and rare capacity. It is the ability to sit inside a stream of small reasonable calls and notice the moment the stream has become a direction, before the direction calcifies into identity. You cannot install it with a framework, because a framework is just another tripwire, and drift does not trip wires. You build it the way you build any judgment that lives below the level of policy. You put people inside situations where the real decision is buried in the noise, and you watch whether they reach for it or wait for it to raise its hand.

That is the uncomfortable thing SSUNDAR's simulations tend to surface. Under pressure, with crises stacking and a clock running down, most capable leaders manage the decisions that present themselves cleanly and miss the one that never does, the assumption that quietly became the plan while they were busy being competent about everything else. The pattern is remarkably consistent, and it has very little to do with intelligence. It has to do with whether a leader has ever been taught to distrust the most dangerous sentence in corporate language. This is just how we do things.

The most expensive decisions in your company were never made. They were allowed.
