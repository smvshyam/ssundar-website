CAPABILITY ARCHITECTURE

Domain Experts Are Protocol Thinkers, Not Systems Thinkers.

AUGUST 12, 2026 9 MIN READ

The restructuring slide is elegant. On the left, the old shape of the organisation, a wide base of people who moved transactions from one state to another, keyed the entry, chased the exception, closed the ticket. On the right, the new shape, narrow and expensive, a small core of deep domain experts sitting on top of a compute layer that now does the moving. The logic is sound and the arithmetic is better. You retire the tier that was only ever executing steps, you keep the people who actually understand the domain, and you let the machines run the steps at a speed and cost no human tier could match. Everyone in the room nods, because it is the right call. Then someone says the quiet part with real confidence. Now we finally have a leadership bench that can think about the whole system. And that sentence, delivered as a conclusion, is where the trouble starts, because it does not follow from anything on the slide.

What leadership believes it has just purchased is systems-thinking capacity. What it has actually retained is the deepest protocol thinkers in the building. These are not the same asset, and the gap between them will not show up in this quarter, or the next. It will show up eighteen months later, when the newly promoted domain experts have had every opportunity to redesign the system they now own, and the system looks exactly as it did when they inherited it, only run by fewer people who are more tired.

Depth Is Not The Same Wiring As Range.

It helps to be precise about what a domain expert is genuinely brilliant at, because the brilliance is real and this is not an argument against expertise. A domain expert has mastered a protocol. There is a body of accumulated knowledge, a set of known states the process can be in, a set of correct moves for each state, and a hard-won instinct for the edge cases the manual never covered. Give this person a supply chain, a tax position, an underwriting file, a clinical pathway, and they will run it better than almost anyone alive, catch the error three steps before it compounds, and know exactly which exception is safe to wave through and which one is the start of a very bad month. That is protocol thinking, and at its highest level it looks so much like general intelligence that it is easy to mistake for it. It is not general intelligence. It is a very deep, very narrow competence in executing a bounded process whose rules already exist.

Systems thinking is a different wiring entirely, and it is built by different means. It is the capacity to see how functions collide, to notice that the local optimum one team is being celebrated for is quietly sabotaging the whole, to read the signal the market is sending before the historical data gets around to confirming it. The protocol thinker asks whether the process was run correctly. The systems thinker asks whether the process should exist in its current shape at all, given what everything around it is doing. One works inside the frame with extraordinary precision. The other works on the frame, and is comfortable with the fact that the frame has no manual, no known states, and no correct move waiting to be looked up. You can be world-class at the first and have never once done the second, and nothing in your ten years of depth will have forced you to.

That last point is the one organisations refuse to sit with. Depth does not accumulate into range. Stacking another year of domain mastery on top of nine does not, at some threshold, quietly convert into the ability to redesign the domain. The two capacities are laid down by different kinds of work. Protocol mastery is built by repetition inside a stable set of rules until the rules become instinct. Systems capacity is built by being made to reason across boundaries where no stable rules exist, where the answer depends on a dozen moving functions at once, and where the honest response to most questions is that it depends and here is on what. An organisation that has only ever rewarded and measured the first has no reason to expect the second to appear, and every reason to expect it to be absent, because it selected against it for a decade without noticing.

The Promotion That Guarantees A Frozen System.

So watch what happens next, because it is almost mechanical. The transactional tier is gone. The domain experts are the most senior people left who actually understand how the work is done, which makes them the obvious, the only, candidates for the architecture roles, the seats where someone is now supposed to decide what the system should become rather than merely run what it is. They are promoted for their depth. They are then asked, on the strength of that depth, to do a thing their depth never trained them for. And they do what any brilliant protocol thinker does when handed a problem outside the protocol. They reach for a protocol. They optimise the process they already know. They make the existing system faster, cleaner, more thoroughly documented, more defensible, and they do it superbly, and none of it changes the shape of the thing, because the shape of the thing was never a protocol question.

You can see it in the artefacts they produce. The best domain expert promoted into an architecture seat will hand you a magnificent map of the current state, exhaustive, correct to the third decimal, every step and hand-off and exception accounted for. What they will struggle to hand you is a future state that is meaningfully different from the present one, because imagining a genuinely different arrangement of the whole is not an act of documentation. It is an act of reasoning across collisions they were never asked to look at, from a market signal that has not yet cleared the reporting lag, toward a design that does not exist yet and therefore cannot be looked up. Asked to redesign, they refine. Asked to rethink the frame, they perfect the picture inside it. And because the refinement is genuinely excellent, everyone congratulates them, and the deeper failure hides comfortably behind the visible competence.

The organisation then arrives at its bewildered question. We put our best people in charge of the system. Why does the system never improve. The answer is uncomfortable and it is structural, not personal. The system does not improve because the people in charge of it are magnificent at running it and were never built to redesign it, and nobody in the promotion chain distinguished the two capabilities, because the whole culture had quietly agreed that enough depth was the same thing as range. It never was. A supply-chain master who can execute the protocol flawlessly is not, by that fact, able to redesign the system the protocol sits inside, any more than the finest driver alive is, by that fact, a person who should be laying out the road network. The skills rhyme. They are not the same skill, and the cost of pretending otherwise is an architecture that gets more efficient every year and more obsolete at exactly the same rate.

Systems Thinking Has To Be Built On Purpose.

The way out is not to fire the domain experts and hire a different species, which is the panicked overcorrection organisations reach for once they finally see the problem. The domain experts are irreplaceable at the thing they are irreplaceable at, and you have just spent a restructuring making them the core of the company. The move is to stop assuming systems thinking is a bonus that ships free with seniority, and to start building it deliberately, in the specific people who are going to hold the architecture, as a named capability rather than an assumed one. That means the design-thinking and critical-thinking work that forces a leader to stop performing expertise and start naming the actual capacity a decision requires. It means putting them in front of problems that have no protocol, where the only way through is to reason across functions that are colliding, hold several moving parts at once, and commit to a shape of the whole under real uncertainty, which is the one condition their entire careers were arranged to spare them.

This is the work SSUNDAR does, and most of it happens before anyone draws a new box on the operating model. We treat systems thinking as an architecture problem rather than a talent that was supposed to arrive on its own, because in almost every organisation we see, it was supposed to arrive on its own and it did not. We separate the two capabilities out loud, so that a promotion into an architecture seat stops being a reward for depth and becomes a genuine test of range, and so that the leaders who have the wiring can be found and built rather than assumed into existence. It is slower than reading a title and inferring a competence from it. What it produces is a bench that can actually redraw the frame, and not merely keep the current frame immaculate while the market quietly reshapes the ground underneath it.

The place this distinction stops being a theory and becomes visible is under load. A crisis does not care how deep your protocol runs. It arrives without a manual, in states no checklist anticipated, demanding a read of the whole while the whole is still moving, and it separates the two kinds of thinker in minutes. The protocol master reaches, with total composure, for the nearest known procedure, and executes it beautifully against a problem it was not built for. The systems thinker does the harder, less telegenic thing, holds the collisions in view, names the signal before the data confirms it, and decides on the shape rather than the step. You do not find out which of your leaders is which by reading their résumé, and you certainly do not find out on the day the actual crisis lands. You find out by putting them somewhere the protocol runs out on purpose, and watching what they reach for when it does.

Ten years of depth does not become range at year eleven. Protocol mastery and systems thinking are laid down by different work, and the organisation that cannot tell them apart will keep promoting the person who perfects the picture over the person who could redraw it.

You did not buy systems thinking when you kept your best domain experts. You bought the deepest protocol thinkers in the building, and then you handed them the frame and wondered why it never moved.
