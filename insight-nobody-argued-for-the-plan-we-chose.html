Nobody Argued for the Plan We Chose.

OCTOBER 21, 2026 6 MIN READ

There were two plans on the table and both of them were right. One team wanted to rebuild the thing properly and slowly. The other wanted to ship the smaller version now and repair it in flight. Each plan was internally coherent. Each had an owner who believed in it with the quiet conviction of someone who had thought it through. Each would have worked on its own terms. The meeting existed to choose between them. It did not. After ninety minutes of the kind of discussion that leaves everyone feeling heard, the room settled on a third plan that sat tidily between the first two. Build most of it, ship some of it, on a timeline that split the difference down the middle. Everyone nodded. The decision felt mature, balanced, grown up. And not one person in that room, caught alone in the corridor afterward, would have chosen the plan they had just agreed to.

This is the compromise, and it is the most respectable method an organization has for not making a decision. It looks like judgment. It feels like fairness. It produces a plan that is the arithmetic mean of two real convictions and the honest expression of neither.

The midpoint is a position nobody holds.

The two original plans were not two points on a line. They were two different theories of the situation. The slow rebuild was a bet that the foundation was the problem and everything else was downstream of it. The fast ship was a bet that speed was the problem and the foundation could wait. These are not positions you can average, because they disagree about what the problem is, not merely about how much of it to do. Splitting the difference between two opposing bets does not give you a moderate bet. It gives you a plan that is too slow to win the race and too shallow to fix the foundation, that spends real rebuild money without buying a rebuild and absorbs real time-to-market risk without buying speed. The halfway plan inherits the costs of both theories and the benefits of neither. Nobody would have designed it starting from a blank page, because it answers a question no one actually asked: what would it look like to be partially committed to two incompatible things at the same time.

And yet the midpoint exerts a pull nothing else in the room can match, because it is the only option that lets everyone leave the table intact. Picking the rebuild means telling the ship team their read of the situation was wrong. Picking the ship means telling the rebuild team the same. Splitting the difference means nobody has to hear it. Both owners keep their standing, both theories survive in fragments, and the plan becomes a treaty rather than a choice. A treaty is a wonderful thing for a relationship and a terrible thing for a strategy, because it is optimized for the comfort of the signatories and indifferent to whether it describes a coherent course of action.

Watch, then, what the compromise is actually optimizing. Not the outcome. The afternoon. It is a plan engineered to minimize friction in the forty minutes it takes to agree on it, and the fact that it will underperform quietly across the following year is a cost charged to a later quarter and a different conversation. The room trades a hard moment now for a soft failure later, and calls the trade balance. The people who built the two real plans walk out faintly dissatisfied, which the room reads as proof of fairness, because if everyone is a little unhappy the decision must have been even handed. Shared mild disappointment is mistaken for justice. It is usually just evidence that the plan serves no one.

The deepest cost arrives only when the compromise fails, and it is a cost the two original plans would never have imposed. When a slow rebuild fails, you learn the foundation was not the problem. When a fast ship fails, you learn speed was not enough. Each failure is legible. Each one teaches the organization something it can use next time. The halfway plan teaches nothing, because when it fails you can never separate the two possible lessons: was the idea wrong, or did you simply do half of it and starve the half you did? A compromise fails ambiguously, and ambiguous failure is the most expensive kind, because the organization pays the full price and receives none of the learning.

The room did not decide. It declined to.

Here is the inversion the room cannot see about itself. It believes it made a decision and selected the middle plan. It did the opposite. The compromise is precisely what a group reaches for when it cannot bear to decide, because deciding would mean telling one of two respected colleagues that their theory of the situation lost. The midpoint is the escape hatch from that single uncomfortable sentence. It is not the resolution of the disagreement. It is the disagreement, preserved whole and sealed inside a plan, where it will keep doing its work out of view. The organization did not choose a path. It chose to not choose, then staffed and funded the not choosing, and will spend a year discovering that an unmade decision does not stop being a decision just because no one was willing to make it.

A compromise is two people agreeing to a plan on the shared condition that neither ever has to admit the other was closer to right.

Rebuilding this does not mean banning compromise, which would be its own kind of foolishness. Some disagreements genuinely resolve into a synthesis, a third plan that beats both originals because it integrates what each side actually saw. That is real, and it is rare, and it looks nothing like splitting the difference. The difference between the two is a test almost no room applies. Can anyone argue for the chosen plan on its own merits, as the best available option, without once mentioning that it keeps the peace? A genuine synthesis has an advocate who will defend it standing alone. A lazy compromise has only the absence of objectors. If the strongest thing anyone can say for the plan is that everybody can live with it, the room has not found its answer. It has found its exit, and mistaken the two.

This reflex is nearly impossible to catch in the moment, because reaching for the middle does not feel like avoidance. It feels like wisdom. We built the simulation to make it visible, to put leaders inside a decision where two sound options pull hard in opposite directions while the clock runs down. The pattern surfaces almost every time. Under pressure, a certain kind of leader will reliably drift to the midpoint, present it as balance, and feel most reasonable at the exact moment they are deciding least. The simulation does not grade whether a leader chose the fast plan or the slow one. It reveals whether, when the room was aching for the comfortable average, the leader could still make an actual choice and own which bet they were placing.

So look at the plan on your table and ask the only question that matters: who in this room would have chosen it alone. If the answer is no one, you are not looking at a decision. You are looking at a truce.
