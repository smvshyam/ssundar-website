We Owned the Decision. Nobody Owned the Assumption.

OCTOBER 12, 2026 5 MIN READ

The review was thorough, which was the first sign something was wrong. Six weeks after the number came in at a fraction of what anyone had modeled, the organization did what competent organizations do. It convened a room, built a timeline, and walked the decision backward from the outcome. Who approved what. When. On whose recommendation. The governance held up beautifully. Every signature was in place, every gate had been cleared, every stakeholder consulted. The process had worked exactly as designed. And the result was still a quarter of what the plan had promised.

So the room kept digging into the decision, because that is where organizations are trained to look. Was the approval too fast. Was the wrong committee in the chair. Should the threshold have been higher. Nobody opened slide four. Slide four was eighteen months old. It held a single line, stated once at the start and never spoken of again, that demand would hold while they built. Everything after slide four, every model, every milestone, every approval the room was now autopsying, was arithmetic performed on top of that one sentence. The decision was never the fragile part. The sentence beneath it was. And the sentence had no name next to it.

A decision is an event. An assumption is a condition.

This is the quiet structural fact that decision reviews are built to miss. An organization knows how to own a decision. A decision is an event. It has a date, a decider, a document, a line in the minutes. It can be praised, blamed, reversed, reconstructed. Accountability attaches to it naturally because it has edges. An assumption has none of these. It is not an event but a condition, and conditions do not announce when they change. They simply stop being true, quietly, while the plans built on top of them keep moving at full speed.

So the two inputs that together produce every outcome get radically different treatment. The decision gets a RACI chart. The assumption gets a bullet point. The decision is tracked in a system of record. The assumption lives on a slide that was presented once and filed. The decision has an owner who will be asked about it at a performance review. The assumption has a drafter, which is not the same thing, because the drafter stated it and moved on and was never again responsible for whether it stayed true. The single most consequential input to the decision is the one input nobody was assigned to watch.

Look closely at who actually produced the assumption and the inversion is almost funny. The belief that demand would hold, or that the talent would stay, or that the integration would be clean, was very often first written by whoever sat closest to the model. An analyst. A planner. Someone two or three levels below the people who would later approve the decision and carry its consequences. The organization put its most senior, most accountable people in charge of the call, and left its most important input in the hands of whoever happened to be holding the spreadsheet. Not through negligence. Through a structure that treats an assumption as an input to be gathered rather than a bet to be owned.

Assumptions, unlike decisions, are also inherited. A number gets set in one planning cycle on the basis of a belief about the market. The next cycle inherits the number, not the belief. By the third cycle the belief has vanished and only the number remains, carried forward as fact, defended as if it were data, when it was always a wager somebody once made about the future and never revisited. Organizations relitigate their decisions constantly. They almost never re-audit the assumptions those decisions were priced on, because an assumption, once it hardens into a plan, stops looking like a question at all.

There is a particular comedy in watching a mature organization respond to this. The outcome misses. The instinct is to add rigor to the decision. A new approval layer. A tougher business-case template. A pre-mortem workshop. Every one of these improvements operates on the decision, the part that already had an owner and a paper trail, and leaves the assumption exactly where it was, unowned and unwatched, free to be wrong again next quarter inside a different plan. The organization has mistaken the visible half of its judgment for the load-bearing half. It keeps reinforcing the beam that was never going to fail and ignoring the ground it stands on.

The decision was never the risky part.

A brilliant decision built on a stale assumption fails exactly as hard as a careless one. The quality of the decider stops mattering the moment the belief underneath has quietly expired.

Which is the uncomfortable part. In most consequential decisions the choice itself carries very little independent risk. It is close to fully determined by the assumptions feeding it. Give a competent team an accurate picture of the world and the call becomes nearly mechanical. Give them a confident, unexamined belief about the world and it no longer matters how good they are. So the real decision was made earlier, and quietly, at the moment someone judged which assumptions were safe enough to leave unquestioned. That was the position that owned the outcome. It is a position almost no organization staffs.

Name the belief. Give it an owner and an expiry.

Rebuilding this is not a motivational exercise about challenging your assumptions, a phrase said in enough offsites to mean nothing. It is structural. Every plan of consequence rests on two or three load-bearing beliefs. Name them, literally, as a short list held separate from the decision they support. Give each one an owner who is not the person who wrote it, and whose only job is to watch whether it still holds. Give each one an expiry date and a trigger: the specific signal in the real world that would prove it false, and a standing instruction to raise a hand the moment that signal shows up. The goal is not to be right about the future. The goal is to know, in real time, the instant you have stopped being right, while there is still a decision left to change.

This is also the pattern that is nearly invisible from the inside and unmistakable from the outside. When we watch leaders work a cascading decision under pressure, the thing that separates the strong ones is rarely the quality of any single call. It is whether anyone in the room is tracking the belief the whole chain is standing on, and notices the moment it gives way. Most do not. They defend the decision long after the assumption underneath it has expired, because the decision is the thing with their name on it. The assumption never had a name at all. That is what the simulation is built to surface: not whether your leaders can decide, but whether anyone is watching the ground they are deciding on.

Every failed decision has an owner. The assumption that doomed it almost always died an orphan.
