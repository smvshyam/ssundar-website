01 · The leak

“It takes four months before a new hire is any use.”

You hire 100 for a requirement of 80. The loss happens before anyone is measuring.

The shortfall surfaces in a QBR as a line about refreshers and backfill, after the client has felt it. By then the penalty is booked and the team leaders have spent a quarter firefighting. The name for it is time to proficiency, and it is forecastable.

NeverSome never reach the floor. Offer-to-floor loss is absorbed as a hiring number, not a capability number.
LateSome reach it late. The seat is paid for weeks before it earns; graduation and proficiency are not the same day.
GoneSome leave before they are dependable. New-hire attrition is reported with tenured attrition, so its cause stays hidden.

05 · The method

“A forecast nobody acts on is just a scoreboard.”

Three loops that make the forecast usable.

A number on its own changes nothing. It changes something when a team leader can act on it this week, and when the floor trusts it enough to tell the truth early. Three loops run underneath Capability Forward, and they hold each other up.

CapacityMost capacity thinking in this sector begins and ends with the new ramp: will this requisition hit threshold headcount. That is half the account. The other half is the steady-state business — who on the floor today is drifting toward an exit, a performance plan or a capability gap, and how far out that can be seen. Both are forecast, then rolled into one account view: if nothing changes, this is the gap you and the client are looking at next quarter.New ramp and existing business, same discipline
CapabilityEvolution, not elimination. As the simplest work is automated, the real risk is losing the tribal knowledge along with the routine tasks. So every person in the forecast carries a paired view: what is being automated out of the role, and what they are moving up into — coaching, the harder cases a model cannot resolve, the judgment calls. Those calls get written into a shared playbook, so a reason code that lives in one team leader’s head today survives them moving on, up or out.The playbook is the asset, not the training calendar
SafetyThe objection to showing people where they stand is that it breeds anxiety — or that they disengage because a model has decided their fate. The answer is not to hide the forecast. A person is only ever at risk for one reason: falling meaningfully short of a target they already knew about. They see their own trajectory before anyone else does, and are asked what help they need rather than told what the consequence will be.Accountability and safety are not in tension here

The habit that earns the next mandate.

Every durable client relationship follows the same arc. Trust starts on paper. It gets verified when a ramp actually runs. It is reinforced or eroded through business as usual. Only then does it open into growth. Capability Forward is built to win at every stage of that arc, not just the first.

The mechanism is a habit rather than a model: declare the plan before the client asks for it, and bring bad news before the client finds it. At kickoff, the 90-day forecast and the quarterly glide path go to the client up front — here is where we are, here is where we are headed, here is what could move it. When the forecast flags a real risk, you are the one raising it, with a fix and a date attached, rather than the client calling to ask what happened. That single habit, reliably executed, is what turns a vendor-management relationship into one where you are asked to take on more scope, not audited to keep what you have.

07 · Questions operations leaders ask

Before the first conversation.

What is time to proficiency, and why does it decide margin?

Time to proficiency is the gap between an offer and a hire who can carry the work unsupervised. It has two halves most operations report as one: offer-to-floor, which ends at graduation, and the days after graduation before the seat actually earns. An account is paying for those seats the whole time, so the length of that window — not the headcount number — is what decides whether the quarter makes margin.

How do you forecast a bench 90 days out?

Per account and per cohort, from signals the operation already generates: dialer statistics, quality scores, attendance and assessments. Tenured attrition, new-hire attrition and seats still in ramp are modelled separately rather than reported as one number, so an existing-business gap is visible as early as a ramp gap. The output is delivery-ready seats against seats needed at 30, 60 and 90 days, with an honest uncertainty band and the first week the bench falls below 90% of need.

What data do you need, and does anything leave our environment?

One extract from systems you already run. No new platform to host, no audio stored, and no employee-facing step without your legal sign-off. Indicator scores are kept; transcripts are read only where signals disagree.

Who owns this — operations or L&D?

Operations. The diagnosis is priced against the cost of seats paid for but not yet productive, so it buys on the operations budget rather than the learning budget. Heads of L&D are usually the champion and the route in, but the number belongs to the COO, the site head or the shared-services lead.

What does the engagement cost and how long is it?

The entry point is a four-week diagnosis on one account: a 90-day bench forecast, attrition modelled by tenure, a baseline your controller signs, and one recommended action with an owner, a date and a number. It is a fixed fee for one account, and the fee is part of the engagement total rather than on top of it. If the diagnosis finds no material gap, we say so and stop.
