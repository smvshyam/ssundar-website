LEARNING CULTURE ESSENTIALS

The LMS Delivered the Course. Nobody Agreed to Learn It.

AUGUST 27, 2026 5 MIN READ

The dashboard was green. Ninety-one percent completion across twenty thousand people, a number that took a platform, a procurement cycle, and a small team of administrators to produce. The learning function presented it to the executive committee with the quiet confidence of a job done. Everyone had been given the content. Everyone had clicked through the content. The line went up and to the right. And then, one quarter later, the capability the whole program existed to build was nowhere to be found in the work, and the same committee wanted to know what had gone wrong with the people.

Nothing had gone wrong with the people. Something had gone wrong with the arithmetic. Somebody had confused the part of learning the organization can deliver with the whole of learning, and built a machine that does the first with great efficiency while pretending it has done the second.

The thirty percent the organization can actually own.

Here is the uncomfortable proportion nobody puts on a slide. The structured, delivered, schedulable part of learning, the classroom, the module, the workshop, the curated path, carries somewhere between a third and forty percent of the distance to real capability. On a generous day. The rest, the majority, the sixty or seventy percent that actually converts exposure into judgment, happens somewhere the organization cannot schedule and cannot see. It happens when a person struggles with a live problem, retrieves what they half-remember, gets it wrong, corrects, and tries again. It happens in the friction. It happens on their own time and their own initiative, or it does not happen at all.

This is not a philosophy. It is a physical constraint that gets more brutal as the number of people rises. You can push content to twenty thousand people with one instance of a platform. You cannot struggle with a live problem on behalf of twenty thousand people. There is no version of that transaction where the effort of learning is transferred from the learner to the institution. The institution can open the door. It cannot walk through it wearing twenty thousand pairs of shoes.

Spoonfeeding is not a generosity you extend to a workforce. At scale, it is a promise you are structurally incapable of keeping, dressed up as care.

So the organization does the part it can do, delivers the content, tracks the completion, and then treats the silence on the other end as a defect in the audience. The learners are disengaged. The learners lack a growth mindset. The learners need a better nudge, a shorter module, a more delightful interface. Every one of those diagnoses locates the failure in the person who did not do the seventy percent, and none of them notices that the seventy percent was never asked for, never named, and never made anyone's explicit responsibility.

The contract nobody wrote down.

There is a contract underneath every functioning learning culture, and it has two signatures on it. The organization commits to build the thirty percent well: the right structure, the right sequence, access to real problems, a path that goes somewhere. The learner commits to the seventy percent: the retrieval, the practice, the deliberate discomfort of operating slightly beyond current competence. Both signatures, or the thing does not stand up.

Most organizations sign their half and then quietly forge the other. They fund the platform, they mandate the completion, they report the number, and they behave as though clicking through a module discharges the learner's obligation. It does not. The completion certificate is a receipt for delivery, not evidence of learning, and everyone in the building has agreed to treat the receipt as if it were the goods. The moment you make completion the metric, you have told twenty thousand people exactly how little is actually required of them, and they are not wrong to believe you.

Notice what this does to the people who would have done the seventy percent anyway. The genuinely hungry learner, the one who reads past the required reading and builds the side project and asks the question that was not on the agenda, gets the same green checkmark as the person who scrubbed the video to the end while answering email. The system cannot tell them apart, because it only measures the part that everyone completes. It has flattened a meritocracy into an attendance sheet, and then it wonders why the attendance sheet does not predict who becomes capable.

The reframe: learning was never a service.

Here is the turn most learning functions never make. Learning is not a service the organization delivers to a passive recipient. It is a meritocracy the learner opts into, and the organization's job is not to spoonfeed the majority into it but to build the conditions under which the people who opt in can go as far and as fast as their appetite allows. The entitlement model, everyone gets the same content, everyone gets the same certificate, everyone is owed the same outcome, is not egalitarian. It is a ceiling. It caps the hungry at the pace of the disengaged and calls the result fairness.

Flip the contract and everything about the architecture changes. You stop optimizing for completion and start optimizing for the seventy percent: real problems put in front of people early, feedback fast enough to correct a live attempt, visibility for the ones who go further so that effort is seen and depth is rewarded. You stop measuring who finished and start measuring who changed. The content becomes the smallest part of the system, the on-ramp, not the destination. And you stop apologizing for asking the learner to carry their own weight, because the alternative is the green dashboard and the absent capability, and you have already run that experiment.

What rebuilding looks like.

An organization that takes the contract seriously does something its peers find almost rude. It makes the seventy percent explicit. It tells people, in plain language, that the module is the beginning of the obligation and not the end of it, that capability is earned in the work and not conferred by attendance, and that the system is built to reward the ones who do the hard, unscheduled, uncomfortable part. It designs for the hungry instead of pandering to the reluctant, and it accepts that a learning culture is a differentiator precisely because not everyone will opt in.

This is architecture, not enthusiasm, and it is the work SSUNDAR does when a learning function realizes its dashboard has been measuring the wrong thing for years. Not a better platform. Not a more delightful module. A redesign of the contract itself, so that the thirty percent the organization owns is built to trigger the seventy percent it cannot, and so that the people who carry their share are the ones the system finds, funds, and moves up. The content was never the problem. The unsigned half of the agreement was.

You cannot spoonfeed twenty thousand people. You were never supposed to. The whole time you were trying, the ones who were going to learn anyway were waiting for you to notice them.
