We Solved the Problem We Could Solve.

OCTOBER 14, 2026 5 MIN READ

The question on the table did not have a good answer, and everyone in the room knew it inside the first ten minutes. It was the kind of problem that refuses to decompose. Too many moving parts, not enough signal, a future that would not hold still long enough to be modeled. So the room did what capable rooms do when a problem will not yield. It found an adjacent problem that would, and went to work on that one instead. By lunch there was a plan, a timeline, and the particular satisfaction of a hard day's thinking. The plan solved a real problem. It simply was not the one that had put the meeting on the calendar.

Nobody decided to change the question. That is the part worth sitting with. There was no moment where someone proposed abandoning the actual problem in favor of an easier cousin. The swap happened in the grammar, one reasonable narrowing at a time, each step defensible on its own and the sum of them a quiet act of avoidance. The room was never less than diligent. It was diligent about the wrong thing, and it never caught the substitution as it happened.

The swap hides inside the vocabulary.

This is the oldest move in organizational life and among the hardest to catch, because it arrives dressed as progress. A genuinely hard problem is ill-structured. It has no agreed definition, no trustworthy data, no method that guarantees an answer. Sitting inside it is uncomfortable, and worse, it produces nothing you can put in the minutes. An easier problem nearby is well-behaved. It has edges. It can be scoped, assigned, tracked, and reported. Put one of each in front of a room under time pressure and it will drift toward the one that pays effort back in visible output, every time, and it will experience the drift as rigor.

The tell is that the two problems share a vocabulary. The real question might be whether the business can still compete in a market that is quietly changing shape. The substitute is which courses to add to next year's capability plan. The same words travel between them. Capability. Market. Readiness. And a chasm sits in the gap. Because the language carries over, the substitution is almost invisible from the inside. Everyone keeps using the right nouns. It feels like the same conversation. It has become a different conversation entirely, wearing the clothes of the first.

What this costs is not the wasted afternoon, which an organization could survive. It is the false completion. Once the room has a plan and a timeline, the original problem gets quietly marked handled in everyone's mental ledger. It drops off the agenda. It stops generating the discomfort that was the only thing keeping it alive. The organization has not merely failed to solve its hard problem. It has done something worse. It has persuaded itself the problem is solved, which is the one condition under which no one will ever go looking for the answer again.

And the people who do this are not weak thinkers. Usually they are the strongest in the building, which is exactly why it works. A room full of capable operators carries a powerful gravitational pull toward anything that can be operated on. Hand that room a solvable problem and it will solve the daylights out of it. The very reflex that makes those people valuable, the instinct to convert ambiguity into action, is the instinct that files a hard question down until it fits the tools already on the table. Competence is not a defense against this. Competence is how it gets delivered.

A clean answer to the wrong question still passes the audit.

A brilliant solution to the wrong problem survives every review an organization knows how to run. The defect is never in the answer. It is in the question the answer was quietly fastened to.

Here is the part that should change how the room is run. The decision the room made was probably a sound one. The analysis held, the plan was reasonable, the logic ran clean from premise to conclusion. Audit that decision on its own terms and you will find nothing to fault, and that is precisely why the audit misses everything that mattered. The failure was never in the quality of the solution. It was in the identity of the problem the solution had been attached to somewhere back before lunch. You cannot detect a problem substitution by inspecting the solution, because an excellent solution to the wrong problem looks, from every angle an organization is equipped to check, exactly like success.

Someone has to guard the question.

Rebuilding this does not call for more analysis. More analysis is the disease. It calls for someone in the room whose only assignment is to hold the original question in its full, unsolved, uncomfortable shape, and to say so out loud the moment the conversation slides off it. Not a facilitator managing airtime. A custodian of the actual problem, with the standing to interrupt a productive discussion precisely because it has turned productive about the wrong thing. Most rooms carry no such role, because the role generates nothing anyone can report at the end of the quarter, which happens to be the entire reason it is needed.

This is also the pattern that stays buried in any normal review and surfaces in seconds the moment the stakes go live. When we put leaders inside a cascading crisis where the real problem is ugly and underspecified and the clock is already running, the revealing behavior is almost never panic. It is the smooth, confident pivot to a cleaner problem the leader knows how to win. They solve it well. They narrate it with conviction. And the crisis they were actually handed keeps compounding in the background, unattended, while the room quietly congratulates itself on the piece it could handle. The simulation exposes the swap because it refuses to let the easy problem exist. There is no tidier version to escape into. There is only the one that will not decompose, and the leader who can stay inside it turns out to be far rarer than the one who can solve something.

No organization is undone by the problem it could not solve. It is undone by the easier one it solved instead, and filed as done.
