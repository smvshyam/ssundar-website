SYSTEMS ARCHITECTURE

The Client Pushed Back on the Rate. We Had No Proof to Push Back With.

NOVEMBER 20, 2026 6 MIN READ

The renewal meeting had an agenda item called "commercial alignment," which is what procurement calls a request for a discount. The client's category lead put one slide on the screen. It showed our rate per head beside a quote from another provider, and the second number was lower. She asked one question, politely. "What are we paying more for?"

The account director had an answer, and it was a good one. Our people were better prepared. They stayed longer. The bench behind them was deeper. Every sentence was probably true, and none of it could be shown. The training records lived in one system, the delivery numbers in another, and the evidence in the room was the account director's own conviction. By the end of the afternoon the rate had moved. Nobody had lied. Nobody had proved anything either, and in a negotiation those turn out to be the same thing.

The rate card priced a seat. The client was buying an outcome.

Look at what a rate card actually contains. A role, a unit, a price. Every head on it costs the same whether that person is in their second week or their ninth month, whether their cohort is climbing steeply or has gone flat. Two providers submit the same shape of document, and the buyer does the only thing a buyer can do with two identical shapes. They compare the one column that differs. Price.

Readiness is the thing that genuinely differs between providers, and it has no column. So it is priced at zero. The organisation has not lost a price war it was forced into. It entered one voluntarily, by never giving its best asset a line on the sheet.

There is a quieter version of the same mistake inside the building. The training function spent the previous year building proficiency into that account. It was booked as cost, because that is the only ledger a training budget has ever been given. It reduced delivery risk and it protected margin, and it never touched the price list. The one investment designed to separate the offer from the competition was invisible at the exact moment the offer was priced. Cost, forever, in every review.

The proof existed. It was in the wrong tense.

Consider what the account director could actually offer when the question landed. Last year went well. Our people performed. The programme was completed. Each of these is a statement about the past, and the past is the one thing every provider claims. A history cannot be verified from outside, so a sceptical buyer discounts it, correctly.

The buyer was not asking about last year. Her worry was about March: will the people on my account then be able to do the work I am paying for? That is a question in the future tense, and she received an answer in the past tense. The gap between the two is where the discount lives.

The evidence on offer was also a snapshot. A pass rate. A count of people certified. Snapshots flatter every provider equally, which is exactly why a buyer has learned to ignore them. A pass rate tells you who cleared a bar on a given day. It does not tell you whether the cohort behind it is still climbing or has stalled just above the line.

A discount is what a claim costs when nobody can check it.

What the same renewal looks like when readiness is reported forward.

Rewrite the meeting. Ninety days before it, the client has already received a single page. For the seats you will need over the next two quarters, here is where proficiency will stand against your threshold on the date each cohort goes live. This cohort is on its curve. That one is flat and will need attention before it reaches your queue. It is a forecast, so it can be wrong, and a forecast that can be wrong is worth more than a reassurance that cannot.

Three mechanics make that page possible without a new system.

First, trajectory rather than snapshot. The page does not report who passed. It reports the slope of each cohort against the cohort before it, drawn from handling and quality data the operation already produces every day. Nobody types it in, so nobody can flatter it. And because it uses the client's own operating data, it is difficult to argue with.

Second, economy by exception. Most of the work is routine and cheap signals will say so. The expensive analysis is reserved for the places where the signals disagree: a cohort whose scores look healthy and whose handling curve has gone flat. Keep the indicator, discard the raw material. It is also why the client's technology team has no objection to hear. There is no new data store and no new burden on their side.

Third, start from the buyer and work backwards. The same forecast is a different sentence at different altitudes. To the delivery owner it is revenue at risk. To finance it is margin. To the client's technology lead it is a promise of no new burden. To the executive sponsor it is a positioning argument for why this provider and not the cheaper one. Same numbers, four conversations, none of them about training.

Then put the readiness on the price list.

This is the step most organisations skip, and it is the one that moves the commercial result. Seats whose proficiency curve has been shown carry a different commitment from seats that have only been staffed. A response window the delivery owner is willing to sign. A ramp date for a new cohort. A level of cover for the exception cases. Verified readiness becomes a tier on the rate card, and the buyer can compare it.

Watch what that does to the question in the room. "What are we paying more for?" becomes "which of these commitments do you want?" The first question is a negotiation. The second is a selection. The discount does not vanish, but it now has to be argued against a thing that exists on paper, with a date and a number beside it.

A claim the author is paid against.

Proof is the last move, and it is the one that makes the rest believable. Carry some of the risk: tie part of the fee to the result the forecast showed. Own the benchmark, so the standard being measured against is stated in advance and is not chosen after the quarter. A buyer cannot audit your adjectives, but they can read a contract in which you are paid less if the curve does not do what you said it would.

Where this breaks.

It is worth being plain about the limits. Some buyers do not want to pay for readiness at all. For commodity work with low switching costs, the rate will keep winning, and a forecast will not change that. Attribution is imperfect: a service miss has many causes, and a proficiency forecast cannot promise a result that also depends on the client's demand spikes, their systems and their policies. Small or newly formed cohorts produce noisy slopes. And showing a client the curve means showing them the weak cohort as well, which is why leaders hesitate. The decision to be transparent has to be made before the first bad week, not during it.

The slide the client put on the screen was one number beside another number. It was the only comparison anyone in the room had been given. Every discount is a claim that someone else got to audit first.
