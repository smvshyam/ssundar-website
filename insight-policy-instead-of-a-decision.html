JUDGMENT DESIGN

Nobody made the call. They wrote a policy instead.

SEPTEMBER 15, 2026 6 MIN READ

Somewhere in your organization there is a rule that exists because one person, on one bad afternoon, made a call everybody later regretted.

Nobody remembers the afternoon anymore. The person moved on, the context evaporated, the specific mess got cleaned up years ago. What survived is the rule. It hardened into a policy, the policy got a number, the number got a page in a handbook nobody opens until they are about to be judged against it. And now a perfectly capable manager is sitting in front of a situation the rule does not fit, reading a sentence written for a problem that no longer exists, and doing exactly what the policy was built to make them do. Nothing.

A policy is a decision that got tired of being made.

Every organization tells itself a flattering story about why it has rules. Consistency. Fairness. Scale. You cannot have four thousand people each deriving the expense limit from first principles, so you write the limit down, and the writing-down gets called maturity.

Some of that is true. Most of it is cover.

The quieter truth is that a policy is frequently what an organization writes instead of trusting someone to decide. It is judgment, exercised once, under conditions its authors could see and you cannot, then frozen and shipped forward in time. The rule outlives the reasoning. And because the reasoning is gone, nobody can actually tell you whether the rule still applies. They can only tell you that it exists, which is a different thing wearing the same uniform.

The handbook does not record why the rule was made. It records that arguing about it is now somebody else's job, on a floor you will never visit.

This is how a company quietly converts judgment into inventory. Each hard call, each expensive lesson, each near-miss gets processed into a clause and shelved. The shelf grows. It looks like accumulated wisdom. It is closer to accumulated fear, catalogued, indexed, and searchable.

The cost hides in the situations the rule was never written for.

Here is where it gets expensive, and here is why the cost never shows up on any dashboard.

Rules are written for the cases the author imagined. Reality keeps producing cases the author did not. A customer with a legitimate problem that falls two days outside the return window. A hire who is obviously right and obviously does not clear one box on the scorecard. A supplier who needs a decision today and the approval matrix routes through someone on leave. In every one of these, a person who knows exactly what the right move is looks at the policy, sees that the policy does not cover it, and defaults to the answer that cannot get them in trouble. The defensible answer. Which is almost never the correct one.

Nobody logs this. There is no field in any system labelled "good decision not made because the rule was silent." The organization sees a clean audit trail and calls it control. What it is actually looking at is a slow, invisible tax on every judgment its best people were hired to exercise and are now quietly declining to.

And the people notice. They always notice first. A manager learns, usually within the first year, that being right is not rewarded and being covered is. So they stop bringing judgment to work. They bring the handbook. You did not hire the handbook. You are paying senior-operator salaries for it to be read aloud.

The reframe nobody in the room wants.

Now the uncomfortable part.

The thing you built for consistency is the thing that made your organization unable to decide. Every rule you added to reduce variance also removed a rep. Judgment is a muscle, and you have spent a decade writing policies that let people avoid using it. Then a genuine crisis arrives, the kind no clause anticipated, and you discover that the room full of experienced leaders has quietly forgotten how to make a call without a page number to hide behind. They are not incompetent. They are deconditioned. You trained the reflex out of them, one well-intentioned policy at a time, and filed the whole project under governance.

What keeping judgment alive actually looks like.

The organizations that get this right are not the ones with fewer rules. They are the ones that know which of their rules are load-bearing and which are just scar tissue.

They can tell you, for any given policy, what decision it replaced and whether that decision still needs replacing. They build rules for the floor, the genuine minimum below which nobody should improvise, and then they deliberately leave the ceiling open, because that is where judgment is supposed to live. They treat "use your judgment" as an instruction they have actually equipped people to follow, not a trap door that opens under whoever guesses wrong. And critically, they know the difference between a decision that should be made once and encoded, and one that has to be made fresh every time because the conditions never hold still.

Most organizations cannot tell you which of their leaders can still do this, because they have never once watched them try under conditions the handbook does not cover. The annual review does not test it. The competency framework does not test it. Real work tests it, but real work is slow, expensive, and only reveals the answer after the decision has already cost something.

That gap is the reason we built the Organizational Crisis Simulation. It drops leaders into cascading situations no policy anticipated, removes the page number they would normally reach for, and watches what they actually do when the rule runs out. Not what they say they would do. What surfaces when there is nothing left to hide behind and a clock that will not stop. It is the one condition under which you find out whether judgment is still in the building, before the day you need it and it is not.

Because the policy manual is not the problem. The problem is that somewhere along the way it stopped being a record of decisions and became a substitute for making them.

A rule can hold a floor. It was never going to hold a judgment. And the day the situation walks in that the handbook did not see coming, you will not find out how good your policies are. You will find out how good your people still are at deciding without one.
