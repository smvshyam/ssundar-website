JUDGMENT DESIGN

He Was Impressive. So We Stopped Looking.

NOVEMBER 3, 2026 5 MIN READ

The initiative was the most important thing the company would attempt that year, and it took the room about four minutes to decide who would run it. Someone said a name. Someone else said, "He turned the supply chain around, he can do this." A third person nodded, because it was true, he had turned the supply chain around. The meeting moved on to the budget, which got the better part of an hour. The hardest choice on the agenda had been settled faster than the catering order, and everyone left the room feeling it had been a decisive, confident call.

It had been a confident call. Whether it was a good one, nobody in that room was in any position to say, because at no point had anyone examined the thing they were actually deciding. They had examined a reputation and assumed the reputation came with a warranty on everything else.

This is how most large bets get placed. Not on an analysis of the specific judgment the bet will demand, but on the glow of a single impressive fact that happens to be attached to a person, a vendor, or a plan. He fixed the supply chain. She came from the firm everyone respects. The deck was the most polished thing anyone had seen that quarter. One attribute quietly does the work of all the others, and the room mistakes the brightness of that one attribute for evidence about everything it never checked.

The glow is not a feeling. It is a shortcut.

When one trait is strongly positive, the mind fills in the rest in the same direction without being asked. The articulate person is assumed to be competent. The competent operator is assumed to be a competent strategist. The strategist who was right last time is assumed to be right about a situation that shares none of its structure. Each inference arrives feeling like an observation. None of it is. The room is not reasoning from the person to the decision; it is reasoning from an adjective.

What makes this so hard to catch is that it does not feel like a bias. It feels like diligence. "He has a track record" is a sentence built to sound like the conclusion of an evaluation, when it is in fact the thing that quietly replaced one. The room believes it weighed the candidate. It read a headline about the candidate and extrapolated the body copy.

And the extrapolation runs in one direction with a cruelty worth naming. The more impressive the single attribute, the less scrutiny everything attached to it receives. A modestly regarded name gets pressed on specifics, because the room feels obliged to earn its decision. A luminous one glides through, because interrogating it would feel almost impolite, like auditing a gift. So the organization reserves its thinnest examination for its largest bets and spends its sharpest questioning on the people and plans it was never going to over-commit to anyway. The money gets three hours of argument. The person who will spend the money gets four minutes and a nod.

The same mechanism runs in reverse, which is how you know it is a mechanism and not a judgment. One visible flaw, a stumble in the presentation, an unfashionable manner, a gap on the timeline, and the glow inverts into a shadow that dims every genuine strength standing behind it. Capable people carrying one bad signal get questioned into the ground. Impressive people carrying one good signal get waved past the gate. Neither is being evaluated. Both are simply being lit, one from the front and one from below.

A reputation bright enough to vouch for someone is bright enough to switch off the room. And attention is the only material a decision is ever made of.

Here is the part that should be uncomfortable. The impressiveness did not inform the decision. It replaced one. The organization did not choose him because it had examined the fit and found it strong; it chose him because the reputation made examining the fit feel unnecessary. The very quality that won him the room is the quality that guaranteed the room would never look closely enough to discover whether he was right for this particular problem, under these particular conditions, this particular time.

What follows is the quietest cost of all. When a halo bet underperforms, the organization almost never traces the failure back to the four minutes in which it was made. It blames the market, the timing, the team he inherited, the thing nobody could have foreseen. To name the real cause would be to admit the room had mistaken a reputation for an analysis, and no room enjoys indicting itself. So the selection error gets laundered into a situational one, the impressive person keeps the glow intact for the next decision, and the organization learns nothing it could actually use. A bias that hides its own failures does not get corrected. It gets re-elected.

Make the glow pay rent.

Rebuilding this has nothing to do with distrusting impressive people. It is about refusing to let impressiveness and fit remain the same question. They are two questions, and they have to be asked separately, in that order, before the reputation is allowed to enter the room and start answering both at once. Three questions do most of the work:

  • What specific judgment does this decision actually require, named out loud before any reputation is in play?
  • What was the track record a track record of, and does the new problem share its rules or merely its scoreboard?
  • Stated in terms someone could check, what about this person or this plan speaks to the thing in front of us right now, not to the thing they were admired for elsewhere?

None of this is a case for handing the important work to the unimpressive. Competence is real, and a strong track record is a genuine signal. It is simply a signal about a prior game, and it has to be carried across to this one deliberately, not assumed to make the journey on its own. The discipline is narrow and unglamorous: let the reputation earn its place in this decision the way a newcomer would have to, one checkable claim at a time. Watch how little of the admiration survives the translation. That shrinkage is not cynicism. It is the decision finally happening.

The difficulty is that the halo does its quietest, most effective work exactly when the pressure is high and the clock is short, which is precisely where no slide deck and no reference check can reach. That is the reason the organizational crisis simulation is built the way it is. Put a luminous reputation and the actual required judgment into the same unfolding scenario, start the clock, and watch whether a leader evaluates the problem on the table or the legend that walked in beside it. The pattern is remarkably consistent, and almost entirely invisible to the people producing it, until they watch themselves produce it with the result still unknown.

The most dangerous person in the room is never the one nobody trusts. It is the one everybody stopped checking.
